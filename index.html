A sleep-specialist referral packet you can actually hand over

A one-line “please see for sleep problems” note often is not enough for triage. This packet pulls together what a referral letter should contain, the questions worth asking at the first appointment, and a short clinic-choice checklist, so you or your primary-care clinician can send a complete brief.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute’s page on sleep studies, and MedlinePlus’s laboratory-test article on the same subject, treat a sleep study as a medical test ordered in a clinical context — not as a walk-in consumer product. MedlinePlus notes that results may include an Apnea Hypopnea Index (AHI), and that if sleep apnea is diagnosed a clinician may recommend lifestyle changes and, often, a CPAP machine. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine describes its accreditation as a standard that sleep centers must meet for staffing, facilities and policies. Those three facts set the tone for this packet: you are preparing for a medical referral, not shopping a gadget.

1. What the referral letter should carry

A guide to what a sleep clinic referral letter should include is blunt: the letter should be specific enough for triage, not a one-line note. Use the list below as a draft your referring clinician can edit, or as a cover sheet you attach to their letter.

  • The reason for the referral in one sentence: diagnosis, treatment, a second opinion, a CPAP review, or help returning to work — the referral guide lists exactly those purposes.
  • The symptoms, how long they have lasted, and whether a bed partner has seen pauses, snoring or gasping.
  • Current medicines and whether the patient already uses CPAP or an oral appliance (the guide says to mention this explicitly).
  • Prior tests and treatment: previous sleep studies, oximetry, thyroid tests, iron studies, CBT-I, CPAP trials or surgery — again from the same list.
  • Relevant other conditions (heart, lung, mood, weight change) and any urgent red flags such as sleepiness while driving.

2. Questions for the first appointment

The companion page of questions to ask at your first sleep appointment suggests asking what the clinician thinks is going on, which test you need and why, how long results will take, what treatment options exist, what it will cost, and who will manage follow-up. It also tells you to ask what you should do while you wait and which symptoms would make the situation more urgent.

Write your answers in the margins so you are not relying on memory later:

  • Which type of study do you recommend, home or in-lab, and why?
  • For sleep apnea, the same guide lists CPAP, oral appliance therapy, positional therapy, weight management and, in selected cases, surgery as the main options — which of those are on the table for me, and in what order?
  • Who do I call if I cannot tolerate a mask, and how soon is a fitting available?

3. Choosing the clinic before you book

A practical checklist for how to choose a sleep clinic says to ask, before booking, whether the clinic is in-network, what a sleep study typically costs after insurance, and whether a CPAP supply shop on site is billed separately. It also notes that wait times vary by area. AASM Sleep Education’s sleep-center directory is the place to check whether a US center is accredited against the Academy’s standards.

Bring this packet, a current medication list, and any prior sleep-study reports. If you usually take a sleep medicine, MedlinePlus says to ask whether you should take it before a study night. This page is a preparation aid, not medical advice.
